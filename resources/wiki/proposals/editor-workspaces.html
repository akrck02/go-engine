2. Editor workspaces: one task at a time

Status: done · Author’s approval: proposal ☑ plan ☑ (2026-10-18, “sigue hasta tener todo implementado”: approved together, while away)

Why

The editor is one screen that does six jobs at once. With a coin chosen, the inspector asks for its place in the level, the cut and timing of its picture, and its code. The top bar offers testing, building and the project. A level designer scrolls past animation settings on every coin. An artist has no screen of their own. A solo developer is offered every job at every moment.

DaVinci Resolve answers this with pages, one screen per stage of the work. The study behind this change is “Editor workspaces”, a private page of the author’s.

Out of scope

  • A code editor inside the editor (Zed is the project’s code editor).
  • Undo and redo, choosing several nodes, sharp pixel art (a go-renderer filter). The first study found them; they belong to their own changes.
  • 3D scenes in Art (models are not cut or animated here). 3D keeps today’s Level view.
  • Documentation chapters: v0.0.1 is not documented yet.

Design

Workspaces in the editor. Editor.Workspace is a small type with the six values, set with SetWorkspace. Entering Play plays the scene, and leaving it stops. View.Build draws three things: the top bar (project, scene, saved state), the chosen workspace’s panels, and the workspace bar with the status line.

Each workspace is its own file of panels (workspace_project.go, workspace_art.go, …). What is shared (tree, inspector sections) stays where it is. Code says “workspace” because “mode” already means 2D or 3D.

Sprites and tilesets as assets (scenefile). They are read and checked like scenes:

  • Sprite{Picture, Columns, Rows, Animations, Start} in *.sprite.json.
  • Tileset{Picture, TileSize, Solid} in *.tileset.json.

A node’s sprite may name a picture (as today) or a sprite file. A tile map’s tileset may name a picture (with its own tile size and solid tiles, as today) or a tileset file. The engine resolves them once, when a scene is built, through its asset cache. An inline sheet still works and wins, so old scenes play.

Alternatives not taken:

  • Moving every sheet into files at once: it breaks scenes.
  • Resources per scene: the coins of two levels would still disagree.

Art edits these files through editor/art.go. It has no interface in it, so it is tested. It makes a sprite or tileset from a picture, sets the cut, and adds, renames and removes animations. It adds a clicked frame, toggles a solid tile, and saves. Its panels:

  • the list;
  • the canvas: the picture with its cut, frames numbered;
  • the cut and collision, on the right;
  • the animation strip with a preview, underneath.

Level gets a Tool (select, paint, erase, fill, hand) instead of the paint checkbox. It also gets an overlay switch for bodies and camera bounds, and the tile map’s cell grid while it is chosen. A flood fill and a palette in the tileset’s own columns join them.

The inspector loses what moved elsewhere:

  • the sheet and animations, now in Art (it shows the sprite, a “Starts with” choice and a button to open it in Art);
  • the tile size and solid tiles, now in the tileset;
  • Go types in behaviour fields;
  • the 3D/2D tab, moved to Project’s scene settings.

Play uses the editor’s play-in-the-view, with Pause, StepFrame and Restart. It adds a target shape and a watch of the chosen node, whose exported behaviour fields are read by reflection. A log is fed by an slog handler the editor installs while playing.

Project, Code and Build are lists and details over what the editor already knows: Project.AssetTree, Project.Behaviours, the scenes’ descriptions (to find uses), and the builds it runs (kept per target).

Icons are named constants in editor/icons.go, one per meaning. A test checks that no two meanings share an icon.

Result

Built. Sprites and tilesets are files of their own. The engine reads them once, and nodes and tile maps point to them. A node may name the animation it starts with, so nodes that share a sprite start differently.

The editor is six workspaces along a bottom bar (keys 1 to 6, in Play too):

  • Level: a tool rail, fill, overlays, a palette in the tileset’s columns, and an inspector that only places (with “Starts with”).
  • Art: the picture large with its cut numbered, frames clicked into animations, a preview, and solid tiles.
  • Play: pause, a step, a restart, a screen’s shape, a live watch of the scene playing, and the log.
  • Project: thumbnails, filters, uses and settings.
  • Code: the behaviours, their fields and methods, their uses, the missing ones, and the output.
  • Build: a card and a log per target.

Icons are constants with one meaning each. The demo’s coins share one sprite file, and its levels one tileset file. It plays on the 3DS from them.

Checked. Every task’s tests pass, with the order shuffled too. Each workspace was captured with the demo open, on the author’s private progress page (docs are not written for v0.0.1). The demo was played in Azahar.

Review. The reviewer’s findings were fixed:

  • The play log gives the log package back, and is only taken when a game starts.
  • Opening a scene or an Art file over unsaved changes is refused.
  • A shared sprite is never written over, and builds save first.
  • Only Level and Play take the view’s input.
  • Play’s tree is the scene playing.
  • Open in Art opens that node’s sprite.
  • A sprite file chosen drops the node’s own sheet.
  • The screen’s shape is offered for 2D games only.
  • Rebuilding’s errors show in Code.
  • Code reads the scenes at most every two seconds.
  • Sprites, tilesets and clearing have icons of their own.
  • New error codes have tests.

Not done. Undo and redo, choosing several nodes, and sharp pixel art (a go-renderer filter), as the proposal said. In a 3D scene, Level keeps its old view.

For a person. Use the mouse: painting and filling, dragging in Select, clicking frames and tiles in Art, the palette. The tests cover the model and one simulated click. Nobody has painted a level by hand yet.
